Adenosine is a purine nucleoside that doubles as a neuromodulator in the central nervous system. It is not stored in vesicles like classical neurotransmitters; instead, it accumulates extracellularly as a byproduct of ATP metabolism. Concentrations rise during prolonged wakefulness and high neuronal activity, providing a chemical signal of **homeostatic sleep pressure**.

## Receptor subtypes

Four G-protein-coupled receptors mediate adenosine signaling: **A1, A2A, A2B, A3**. The two most relevant for sleep/wake regulation and for understanding [[caffeine]] are:

- **A1 receptor** — inhibitory (Gi-coupled). Widely expressed across the brain. Activation hyperpolarizes neurons and reduces release of excitatory neurotransmitters. A1 signaling is essential for the expression and resolution of accumulated sleep need; conditional A1 knockout mice fail to build up sleep pressure after sleep deprivation.
- **A2A receptor** — stimulatory (Gs-coupled), but paradoxically *sleep- promoting* in the relevant circuit. Densely expressed in the striatum and in the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO) of the hypothalamus, where it disinhibits sleep-active GABAergic neurons. A2A is considered the gate for NREM sleep onset.

A1 modulates sleep depth/need; A2A gates sleep entry. Both contribute to homeostatic regulation in region-specific ways.

## Why this matters for caffeine

[[caffeine]] is a non-selective competitive antagonist at A1 and A2A receptors. By blocking them, it prevents adenosine from signaling fatigue — this is the core mechanism of caffeine's stimulant effect. Chronic blockade causes **receptor upregulation**: the brain compensates by expressing more receptors, which is the substrate for [[caffeine-withdrawal]].
