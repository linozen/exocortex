# Gender differences in mental-illness prevalence

12-month [[prevalence]] estimates show a stark gender pattern across most mental-health diagnoses — women diagnosed at higher rates with depression and anxiety, men with substance-use disorders, etc. The naive reading: men and women suffer different conditions at different rates.

The more cautious reading: the diagnostic *pipeline* selects differently for each gender at several stages. Before treating the gender gap as a statement about underlying prevalence, account for at least three mechanisms.

## Three lenses that complicate the naive reading

### Help-seeking and the base rate

The denominator in any diagnostic-rate statistic is people *who get diagnosed by a mental-health professional* — not people with the underlying condition. If women are more likely to seek mental-health care than men (which they appear to be in most Western health systems), the base rate of "person in the diagnostic system" is gender-skewed before any clinical judgement happens. See [[cognitive-illusions]]#base-rate-fallacy for the underlying reasoning error.

### Selection bias in who reaches the clinician

Even among people who do seek care, the subset of men who present to a mental-health professional may be a self-selected non-representative slice — perhaps those whose symptoms are severe enough to overcome stigma, or those whose presentation matches clinician expectations. The sample of "men in front of a psychiatrist" is not a random sample of "men with mental-health symptoms." See [[selection-bias]].

### Clinician priming

Diagnostic training itself reinforces the expected pattern: clinicians learn that depression is more prevalent in women, anxiety more prevalent in women, ADHD historically more prevalent in boys, etc. Trained expectations shape pattern recognition. A clinician primed to see depression in women may flag ambiguous symptoms as depression more readily; the same symptoms in a man may be coded as something else (irritability, substance use). See [[cognitive-illusions]]#priming.

## What this isn't an argument for

It is not an argument that the gender gap is *entirely* artifactual. Sex differences in hormonal physiology, in social-role exposure to stressors, and in coping repertoires all plausibly contribute to genuine prevalence differences. The point is methodological: the *measured* gap conflates a real underlying gap with at least three measurement artefacts, and pulling the real signal out requires careful design — community-sample screening rather than clinic-sample diagnosis, structured interviews that constrain clinician priming, etc.
