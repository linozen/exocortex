> Processes that occupy vast expanses of both time and space, defying the more traditional sense of an "object" as a thing that can be singled out.

— [Concilience Project glossary](https://consilienceproject.org/glossary/), drawing on Timothy Morton's coinage.

Examples Morton gives: **climate change**, **plastic pollution**, **the biosphere itself**, **global capitalism**, **nuclear materials**. Each is real and consequential, but you cannot point at it the way you can point at a rock. It has parts you can touch (a weather event, a plastic bottle, a stock price) but no boundary you can trace.

Three properties make hyperobjects epistemologically hard:

- **Massively distributed** in time (decades, centuries, millennia) and space (continents, planets).
- **Non-locally manifested** — you only ever encounter pieces, never the thing itself.
- **Phased** in ways that exceed normal human temporal experience.

The implication for action: institutions designed around objects with crisp boundaries (markets, nation-states, legal systems) struggle to perceive — let alone respond to — hyperobjects. This is one of the reasons climate response has been so politically intractable: the thing being responded to is, in a literal cognitive sense, the wrong shape for the institutions tasked with responding.
